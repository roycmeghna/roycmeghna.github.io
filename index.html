    MyoText: EMG-Based Typing via Finger Classification & Transformer Decoding

    The idea

    Typing from muscle signals (sEMG) could let people type without a keyboard at all. The catch: EMG is noisy and looks different on every person, so most systems have to be retrained for each new user.

    How MyoText works

    MyoText turns raw wrist sEMG into full sentences with no per-user training. It builds on Meta Reality Labs' emg2qwerty dataset (over 346 hours of typing from 108 people) and mimics how typing actually happens:

    1. Predict the fingers that fired, with a BiLSTM + attention model.
    2. Narrow the letters using keyboard ergonomics.
    3. Rebuild the sentence with a T5 language model.

    Because it never sees a specific user during training, it works on new people straight out of the box.

    How it turned out

    • 1.6% character error rate and 1.8% word error rate.
    • Over 4x better than earlier personalized baselines, with no calibration for new users.
    • First detailed look at the structured, finger-level EMG patterns hiding in emg2qwerty.
    • A step toward silent, hands-free text input for wearables, AR/VR, and assistive tech.
    sEMGBiLSTM-AttentionTransformers (T5)Zero-ShotPythonTensorFlowHugging Face

    Interpretable Hierarchical Machine Learning for Mental Health

    The problem

    Phones leave behind huge trails of behavioral data, which could say a lot about mental health. But that data is noisy, high-dimensional, and varies wildly from person to person, so ordinary models struggle with it.

    What I built

    I-HOPE is a hierarchical model that turns messy raw behavior into readable interaction patterns. Careful feature reduction trims the data without throwing away what predicts well, so the results stay both personal and interpretable.

    How it turned out

    • 91% accuracy on student mental-health outcomes, up from a 70% baseline.
    • A clear path from raw behavior to meaningful indicators, instead of a black box.
    • Per-student explanations for every prediction, using SHAP.

    Distributed Neural Networks for IoB

    The problem

    Smarter wearables need more compute, and compute drains the battery fast. The question was how to run capable AI on a device that has almost no energy to spare.

    What I built

    DistNN splits a neural network between the tiny wearable and a more powerful nearby hub, like your phone, and finds the best point to make that cut. Custom low-precision hardware handles the wearable's share at very low cost.

    How it turned out

    • About 1000x more energy-efficient than running the same AI on a GPU.
    • Roughly 11x less power than recent state-of-the-art wearable AI chips.
    • Still real-time (30 fps) and accurate (0.9 SSIM on image reconstruction).
    • The wearable's share of the model runs at microwatt-level power.

    Self-Supervised Learning with Squeeze-and-Excitation Networks for EEG

    The problem

    EEG matters for brain-computer interfaces and for diagnosing neurological conditions, but real-world recordings are messy, usually unlabeled, and expensive to process on low-power devices.

    What I built

    SSL-SE-EEG pairs self-supervised learning, which learns from unlabeled EEG, with lightweight squeeze-and-excitation networks. Turning the raw signals into structured 2D images makes them far easier for the models to read.

    How it turned out

    • 91% on MindBigData and 85% on TUH-AB seizure detection.
    • Much less dependence on expensive labeled data, thanks to the self-supervised step.
    • More robust to the noise and artifacts that plague real EEG.
    • Low enough overhead to suit real-time, low-power BCI.

    Low Power Wearable ECG via Human Body Communication

    The idea

    Continuous ECG usually means a bulky device you keep recharging. FlexECG skips the battery entirely: it's a flexible patch that harvests heat from your body and sends the ECG through your body itself using human body communication (HBC).

    The hard part

    Body heat provides very little power, and not steadily. Early versions kept browning out mid-reading, so most of the work went into getting the system to run reliably inside that budget.

    How it turned out

    • A custom HBC transmit-and-decode path for ECG signals.
    • Settles at about 20µW of power.
    • Tested across multiple human subjects.
    • Roughly 4x lower power than comparable state-of-the-art systems.

    Electric Field-Based Intrathecal Drug Delivery (IDD)

    The problem

    Intrathecal drug delivery sends medication straight to the central nervous system for long-term pain relief. The hard part is being sure the catheter actually sits in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), not an adjacent layer, without adding cost or complexity.

    The approach

    CSF conducts electricity better than the tissue around it at certain frequencies (tens of MHz). This system uses a galvanic transmitter and a capacitive receiver at electroquasistatic (EQS) frequencies to read that difference and infer where the catheter tip is.

    What the simulations showed

    • Modeled in Ansys HFSS on the realistic “Neva” human-body model.
    • A clear difference in field strength when the transmitter sits in the CSF versus neighboring layers.
    • A way to verify catheter placement for more precise delivery.
    • As far as we know, the first electric-field approach proposed for IDD.
    Ansys HFSSFEMNeva Body ModelEQS FieldsBioimpedance

    AI for Safer Surgery: Predicting Anesthesia Depth

    The problem

    During surgery, knowing exactly how deeply someone is anesthetized keeps them safe and comfortable, and the usual monitors have limits. The question was whether AI could read it from signals you're already recording.

    The approach

    Using ECG and PPG recorded from real surgical patients, I trained deep models (CNNs and LSTMs) to estimate anesthetic depth in real time.

    How it turned out

    • Processed messy surgical ECG/PPG in MATLAB and built the models in Python (TensorFlow/Keras).
    • About 82% accuracy predicting clinical anesthesia depth from ECG features alone.
    • Published in the BSPC journal.
    • This was my first real paper, from my time at Yuan Ze in Taiwan, and the one that got me hooked on health AI.
